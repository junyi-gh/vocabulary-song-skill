# Learning-song design

Use these checks when the output is intended to teach pronunciation and meaning, not merely mention vocabulary.

## Word load

- Beginner: 3–4 words, one common sense per word.
- Intermediate: 4–6 words, one sense plus a collocation.
- Advanced: 5–8 words, but keep the lyric story coherent.

Mixing unrelated difficulty levels increases cognitive load. Prefer words that can share a believable scene. If the user's words are unrelated, use a framing device such as a journey, dream, classroom challenge, or comic mini-story rather than forcing semantic links.

## Random-set cohesion gate

Assign each word one useful role before drafting: character quality, action, object, setting, obstacle, emotional change, or resolution. A role map reveals whether the set can support an actual narrative.

Do not confuse inclusion with integration:

- **Mentioned** means the word occurs but the listener cannot infer its meaning.
- **Integrated** means the lyric context illustrates the selected sense and advances the song.

Every word must be integrated at least once. A second occurrence may reinforce the hook, collocation, or emotional turn. If two words can only be joined by explaining the vocabulary exercise itself, the set is a candidate for a concept-song frame or a split.

Depth comes from a stable theme, concrete images, tension, and change. Preserve those features even when the word list is random; do not write one disconnected sentence per word.

## Repetition pattern

Give each word distinct encounters:

1. first encounter supplies a context clue;
2. chorus encounter gives a clear, rhythmically prominent repetition;
3. later encounter changes the grammatical or semantic frame when natural.

Mechanical back-to-back repetition is useful once in a hook, but it must not be the only exposure.

## Prosody

- Align primary lexical stress with beat 1 or 3 in 4/4 where practical.
- Keep function words on weak beats.
- Avoid melisma on the target word in a first-learning song.
- Do not rhyme a target word with a false vowel or omit a final consonant for convenience.
- Use a slower tempo for consonant clusters and polysyllabic words.

Singing changes vowel duration and pitch. Always pair the song with a normally spoken pronunciation track.

## Minimum learning checks

The package passes only if the learner can:

- hear and repeat the word normally;
- infer or recall the selected meaning;
- identify the stressed syllable;
- see at least one useful collocation;
- retrieve the word without the complete lyric in view.

The quiz should test retrieval rather than simple recognition. A lyric gap-fill should remove the target word, not merely ask the learner to copy it.
